# NSD Cross-Disorder and Comorbidity Test Plan v0.1

Date: 14 September 2026
Status: PRE-COMPUTE DESIGN / HYPOTHESES NOT CONFIRMED

## 1. Purpose

The historical NSD manuscript made a strong claim that psychiatric comorbidity “dissolves” when disorders are treated as coordinates in one stability space. The forward project does not assume that conclusion.

The narrower testable question is:

> Do clinically distinct disorders share reproducible components of a neurophysiological stability architecture while retaining disorder-specific modal, spatial, temporal, and system-level organization?

A second question follows only if the first survives:

> Does that shared architecture explain any part of observed comorbidity better than simpler alternatives?

## 2. Competing explanations that must remain live

Cross-disorder overlap can arise from:
- genuinely shared mechanism;
- shared but non-identical upstream constraints;
- common symptoms;
- medication effects;
- sleep/arousal/vigilance effects;
- age/development;
- site/device effects;
- diagnostic measurement overlap;
- generic illness burden;
- generic changes in aperiodic or band-power structure;
- common stress or fatigue states;
- sampling bias.

The analysis must be designed so that “shared NSD architecture” can lose to these explanations.

## 3. Disorder-entry rule

A new disorder enters the shared analysis only after:
1. its dataset passes provenance and hierarchy audit;
2. the Structural Engine can operate label-blind on its recordings;
3. the healthy Atlas reference is already frozen or versioned independently;
4. native comparator features are available;
5. the disorder-specific question is defined before inspecting the final comparison.

## 4. First-stage phenotype map

For each disorder independently, characterize:
- spectral state;
- periodic/aperiodic structure;
- modal state;
- licensed local scalars if any;
- spatial organization;
- conglomerate/system organization;
- open-channel/refusal profile;
- within-group heterogeneity;
- relation to symptom dimensions where available.

Do not begin with a joint transdiagnostic embedding that can erase disorder-specific structure.

## 5. Shared-versus-specific decomposition

After disorder-specific maps are frozen, compare them using at least four questions:

### Q1 — Shared marginal shift
Do two disorders shift the same feature in the same direction relative to the same healthy reference?

### Q2 — Shared multivariate geometry
Do they occupy overlapping regions in a frozen multilevel feature space beyond what simple periodic/aperiodic features explain?

### Q3 — Distinct internal organization
Even if marginal features overlap, do mode relationships, spatial participation, or conglomerate organization differ?

### Q4 — Shared transition/recovery structure
Where longitudinal/repeated-state data exist, do disorders show similar response/recovery geometry rather than merely similar static values?

A “shared instability class” claim should require evidence beyond Q1 alone.

## 6. Comorbidity analysis levels

### Level CMB-0 — Epidemiological overlap
Document diagnostic co-occurrence rates from high-quality external literature.

Purpose: context only. High comorbidity does not prove shared neurophysiology.

### Level CMB-1 — Symptom-dimensional overlap
Where datasets contain symptom measures, test whether shared neurophysiological features track shared symptoms rather than diagnosis labels.

### Level CMB-2 — Physiological overlap
Test whether comorbid participants occupy positions expected from:
- additive disorder effects;
- one dominant disorder effect;
- a shared latent physiological axis;
- a distinct interaction phenotype.

### Level CMB-3 — Architecture interaction
Test whether comorbidity is better characterized by relationships among layers, such as:
- spectral similarity with spatial divergence;
- shared mode with different participation;
- scalar similarity with different refusal/missing-mode patterns;
- altered coupling between otherwise ordinary local features.

### Level CMB-4 — Longitudinal transition
Only with appropriate data, ask whether the architecture predicts transitions among symptom states or diagnoses.

This is a separate prospective claim and cannot be inferred from cross-sectional overlap.

## 7. Null models

At minimum compare against:
- diagnosis-only model;
- symptom-only model where available;
- standard spectral model;
- periodic/aperiodic model;
- demographics/site model;
- additive disorder model for comorbidity;
- random-label/permutation controls preserving subject/site hierarchy.

## 8. What would count against the shared-instability hypothesis?

The hypothesis narrows or fails if:
- different disorders do not share stable features beyond native baselines;
- apparent overlap disappears after site/age/medication/state control;
- shared features are generic to poor signal quality or vigilance;
- full architecture transfers worse than simple spectral features;
- disorder-specific organization is erased by the proposed common representation;
- comorbid participants are explained adequately by additive/simple models;
- the same healthy reference cannot transfer across disorders without retuning;
- modal/scalar admission rates differ primarily because of recording quality or acquisition method.

## 9. What would support a shared-instability class?

A defensible support pattern would look more like:
1. same frozen Engine across disorders;
2. same frozen healthy Atlas reference;
3. reproducible shared feature or relational structure across independent datasets;
4. preserved disorder-specific differences in other layers;
5. incremental value beyond standard periodic/aperiodic and clinical baselines;
6. replication or external transfer;
7. no dependence on disorder-specific retuning.

## 10. Geometric analysis safeguards

State-space plots can be seductive. Therefore:
- axis definitions must be frozen before final plotting;
- embeddings such as PCA/UMAP/t-SNE are descriptive unless validated for the claim;
- no manual placement of disorders in coordinates;
- no plotting synthetic “patients” alongside empirical subjects without unmistakable labeling;
- density/cluster claims require uncertainty and stability analysis;
- visually separated clusters do not establish a mechanistic class.

## 11. Comorbidity language ceiling

Until data support more, preferred language is:

> “NSD tests whether comorbid and non-comorbid phenotypes can be represented within a shared multilevel neurophysiological architecture while preserving distinct organization.”

Avoid:
- “comorbidity paradox is solved”;
- “you cannot be comorbid with yourself” as a scientific conclusion;
- “all mental illness is one disorder”;
- “diagnoses are irrelevant.”

Those historical formulations exceeded the available evidence and are not part of the forward claim set.

## 12. First cross-disorder sequence

When data are ready, the preferred sequence is:
1. healthy reference;
2. ASD or other already-audited first phenotype;
3. second disorder with materially different clinical architecture;
4. shared-versus-specific decomposition;
5. third phenotype only after the comparison rules remain frozen;
6. comorbid subgroup analysis where source data support it;
7. external transfer.

The sequence is designed to prevent the framework from changing shape every time a new diagnosis is added.
